# Review against the paper

**Paper:** R. Marbel, B. Ben-Moshe, R. Yozevitch, "Star-Tracker Algorithm for Smartphones and Commercial Micro-Drones", *Sensors* 20(4):1106, 2020. doi:10.3390/s20041106

**Scope of the paper:** camera **orientation** from a night-sky image. Its expected accuracy is 0.1–0.5°, and about one pixel (≈1/50°) on calibrated phones (§1.2, §4.3). The paper does **not** compute a geographic position. It does not use the accelerometer, and it does not discuss image stabilisation. StarLocator's position step (gravity + clock → latitude/longitude) is an addition built on top of the paper's orientation.

(3) | Pixel distance = S × angular distance (one linear scale) | Each pixel is turned into a 3-D ray through the pinhole model (focal length, principal point, radial distortion), and angles are measured between rays | Deliberate improvement. A single scale is off by several percent at the edge of a 60–80° field (the paper's §3.1.2 notes the distortion problem) |
| §2.1.1 | Two identified stars give full registration; triplets are matched because of outliers | Triplets are looked up; each orientation hypothesis is built from two labelled stars (TRIAD) | Matches |
| §3.1, SPHT | Every catalog triplet that fits in the field of view (magnitude limit "often 5"), keyed by its three sorted, rounded distances; value = the three star names | Triplets of stars brighter than mag 4.0 with sides 1–30°; key = three sorted quantised distances; value = the three stars in opposite-side order (gives the vertex correspondence) | Matches. Mag 4.0 instead of 5 keeps the table small (518 stars, 94,439 triangles). Fainter stars (to 5.5) are still used in validation |
| §3.1.1, AL rounding | Rounding by an "accuracy level" AL. A distance near a rounding boundary can fall in the neighbouring key, so the paper lowers AL (more triangles per key) to compensate | Bin width = 2 × tolerance (0.4°). Every query visits all bins its ±tolerance interval touches, then checks each side exactly | Improvement. No triangle within tolerance can be missed because of a bin edge, which is the weakness the paper's Fig. 5 measures |
| §3.2, Alg. 2 + 4, confidence | Each frame triangle pulls catalog triplets; each star collects candidate labels; confidence = how often the most frequent label appears (normalised in eq. 6) | Each matched catalog triangle votes for its three labels, weighted 1/(number of catalog matches of that frame triangle). A star is labelled if its best label has ≥ 0.5 votes and beats the runner-up by 20% | Same idea (votes over triangle intersections). The 1/n weighting stops an ambiguous triangle from counting as much as a unique one |
| §3.3, Alg. 3, validation | Transform the frame with an orientation from ≥ 2 stars; nearest catalog neighbour for every star; drop pairs that are too far apart; RMS = error estimate; among conflicting orientations keep the smallest error | TRIAD per pair of labels; each hypothesis is scored by how many other labels agree; nearest-neighbour matching of all detections within 0.35°; RMS reported | Matches, with stricter acceptance: ≥ 4 independent labels must agree, the match count must beat chance by 5σ, and the RMS must be under half the tolerance. The paper's nearest-neighbour search also uses magnitude as a third dimension, and its RMS is confidence-weighted. Both are omitted here because the position check is already tight |
| §3.3, refinement | Gradient descent minimising the RMS (point-cloud registration) | Closed-form least squares over all matched stars (Davenport q-method, the exact minimiser), iterated with re-matching | Equivalent result, exact instead of iterative |
| §3.1.2, calibration | Radial distortion matters at wide FOV; star distances can be used to calibrate after a first identification | Focal length and K1 are refined from the identified stars. A wide focal-length sweep handles phones that report wrong intrinsics; the learned scale is saved per camera | Matches the paper's suggestion |
| §4.4, accuracy test | Leave one star out, predict where it should be, compare | Fit RMS per star 0.036–0.06° on 36 real frames (60–80 stars each) | Within the paper's 0.1–0.5° and near its "≈1 pixel" figure |

## Findings

1. **The paper's algorithm is implemented faithfully.** Where StarLocator differs, the change fixes a stated weakness: the cosine in eq. (2), the linear scale of eq. (3), and misses at the edges of rounding bins. Or it makes acceptance stricter.
2. **Real-frame result:** 36 of 36 frames from two nights identified 61–80 stars at 0.04–0.06° RMS. That is as good as or better than the paper's own figures.
3. **One weakness was found and fixed in 1.0.7.** When the phone's reported focal length is far off and the wide focal sweep is used, a weak chance match (12 stars, RMS 0.18°) could pass. Now a match needs RMS ≤ ½ tolerance, and a wide-sweep match needs twice the normal number of stars. With the learned focal correction (the normal case in the app) the frame already solved correctly (77 stars).
4. **What the paper cannot detect.** Its accuracy test (§4.4) compares stars within one image, so it measures where the image points, not where the phone body points. That is enough for orientation. For position, the image must also be fixed to the accelerometer. Field test 2 showed it was not, because of image stabilisation, so StarLocator adds its own image-stability check (`PositionSolver.ZenithSpread`). The paper's remark (§5) that drone cameras are gimbal-stabilised is the same issue in another form: a stabilised camera reports the camera's direction, not the body's.
